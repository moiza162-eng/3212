Email

Write a welcome sequence people can act on

Use the promise made at signup

Open the first email with what the person asked for. If they joined to get a course guide, link to that guide prominently and explain what they can expect from future messages. Use a recognisable sender and a subject line that accurately describes the email.

Give each message one primary action

A second email could answer the most common decision questions about entry requirements, delivery and the weekly commitment. A third could invite readers to register interest when they are ready. Decide when to stop or change the sequence. For example, someone who already registered may need joining instructions instead of another registration invitation.

Judge the sequence by meaningful behaviour

Check link clicks, replies, completed actions and unsubscribes. Treat opens as a rough diagnostic because mail clients can load tracking images automatically. Test every link, the plain-text version and the unsubscribe route before sending. Review performance by message and change one element at a time so you can connect the change with what you observe.

Map the first week from the reader's perspective

Consider what people know at the moment they sign up and what they need to learn in order to act. Someone who asks for a prospectus may still be comparing options. The first message can deliver the prospectus and point to a clear contact route. A later message can help them decide whether the programme fits. Write one sentence for the purpose of each email and check whether the messages build on one another. If two messages ask the same thing without adding any useful detail, combine them or use one to answer a practical question from the admissions team.

Choose timing and exit rules

Plan the spacing around the decision the reader faces. Send a requested resource promptly and give people room to read it before sending a follow-up. If you mention an enrolment deadline, confirm it with the team that manages admissions and think about what someone will receive if they join after that deadline. Document when to exit the sequence, such as when the reader registers, opts out or is no longer eligible for the offer. If a separate system records registrations, check that it informs the sequence reliably before relying on the rule. Try the flow with a test contact that changes status halfway through.

Write for someone skimming on a phone

Put the most useful information in the first few lines. A short heading, one main action and one clarification usually do more than several competing buttons. Use descriptive link text and make sure important information is available as text rather than embedded in an image. Give readers a usable reply address or make the contact route clear. If you use personalisation, provide a fallback for missing fields and check that the message still reads naturally. Send previews to more than one mail client and read the plain-text version. Test the links as a recipient would and check that they lead to the intended live pages.

Learn from the messages people act on

Compare performance for each email in the sequence and note who received it. People who reach the third message may differ from people who receive the first if registration or an unsubscribe removes contacts earlier. That makes raw click totals hard to compare without context. Look at completion of the primary action as well as unsubscribes and replies. If people repeatedly ask about eligibility after reading the second email, test a clearer explanation and see whether those questions decrease. Keep the strongest version and record what changed. Revisit the sequence when the offer, team contact details or intake dates change. That review can prevent a useful automated email from quietly becoming an outdated one.

Give someone responsibility for checking the whole journey from the signup form to the final message. They should verify that the original promise still matches what the sequence delivers and that each message can stand on its own if the reader missed an earlier email. Keep a short checklist with the automation. Check it after changing a link, updating an intake date or handing the sequence to another team. Test the complete sequence again before the next intake opens.
