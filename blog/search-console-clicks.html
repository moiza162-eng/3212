Why Google Search clicks fall when impressions rise

Check what changed in the mix

A page can appear more often while getting fewer clicks if it starts showing for broader queries. Compare equivalent periods in Search Console and examine queries and pages separately. Look at branded and non-branded terms, then check device and country where you have enough data. Avoid reading too much into a small sample or a holiday week.

Diagnose the affected pages

Suppose course pages are steady but a new guide contributes many impressions and few clicks. That differs from a decline on an established registration page. For the latter, check whether its position changed for the queries that used to drive traffic. Review what the search result promises and whether the page answers that need.

Test one change at a time

If a result looks vague for an important query, revise the title so it states the offer and the intended audience. Keep a note of the date and compare click-through rate, clicks and impressions once there is enough comparable data. Check enquiry volume separately. Search Console describes search visibility, while your site analytics can help explain what visitors did after arriving.

Decompose the headline numbers

Imagine impressions rising from 10,000 to 15,000 while clicks fall from 600 to 570. The aggregate click-through rate moves from 6% to 3.8%, but that alone gives no cause. Find the pages with the largest click losses and the pages contributing most of the new impressions. They may be different. A new article could account for the extra impressions while a mature service page loses clicks. Write those observations separately so the article is not blamed for the service page's performance. Compare the same number of weekdays and include the prior year where seasonality matters and there is enough history. Check the reporting filters before doing any calculations.

Inspect the highest-impact queries

For each page losing clicks, start with query themes that previously sent meaningful traffic. Filter to a page and compare the same searches where the tool provides enough detail. Ask whether the page is being shown less often, appearing in a different mix of positions or earning fewer clicks from similar impressions. Average position can mask substantial variation, so use it alongside other signals. Review the search results as a reader. New competitors, prominent search features or a changed result title can alter what the reader sees. A single manual search is only a snapshot, so record what you observed and avoid treating it as universal.

Check changes on the website

Review the release log for title edits, redirects, page removals or a new navigation structure. A changed URL can make a page-level comparison misleading if the old and new URLs are examined separately. Use Search Console to inspect the pages you expect Google to show. Compare mobile and desktop where useful and check whether the decline is isolated to a particular device or market. If site analytics also shows a decline, look at the landing pages and journeys affected. Search Console clicks and analytics sessions use different definitions. Use consistent measurements within each tool and look for agreement in the direction of change.

Choose the smallest response that fits the evidence

If a relevant page keeps its impressions but the title no longer describes the current offer, test a clearer title and make sure the visible heading supports it. If an important page lost visibility after a move, investigate the old and new URLs before changing editorial copy. If the only shift is growth in impressions for a broad informational article, leave its core content in place and check whether readers have a useful internal link to the next step. Agree a date to review the chosen hypothesis. Record the page, query group and metric you expect to change, then check the result over a comparable period. If the evidence remains mixed, say so and document the additional data you would need.

Keep a dated copy of the original comparison with the filters applied. When you investigate again, use those same settings before trying a new breakdown. This makes it much easier to see whether the pattern genuinely changed or simply looks different because you selected another group of searches or pages.
