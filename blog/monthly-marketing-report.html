Reporting

Write a monthly marketing report that leads to decisions

Begin with the decision on the table

Work backwards from the decision a manager needs to make, such as whether to focus next month's effort on course discovery or registration. Summarise the relevant trend in a few sentences. Define the metric and comparison period so another person can reproduce your conclusion.

Put unusual changes in context

Show a short time series, then explain what may have affected it: a new cohort launch, different paid spend or a tracking change. If registrations increased when both search traffic and advertising activity rose, describe what you can attribute and what needs further analysis. Small counts are often clearer as raw numbers alongside percentages.

Close with owners and checks

Choose two or three concrete actions and name the person and the date responsible for each. For example, ask the web team to improve the eligibility section before the next campaign and have analytics confirm the registration event after release. At the next review, check whether the actions shipped, what evidence appeared and whether the original decision should change.

Give each metric a job

Choose a handful of numbers that show how people move from discovery to a business outcome. A training provider might report non-brand search clicks, visits to course pages, completed enquiries and the number of enquiries that meet enrolment criteria. Write down how each figure is counted, where it comes from and what is outside its coverage. For example, a form report might exclude calls and walk-in enquiries. Use the same definitions in future months. If a tracking change makes a comparison unreliable, include the point at which the method changed and describe which periods can still be compared fairly.

Break down the headline before explaining it

Suppose enquiries rise while the proportion of suitable enquiries falls. Compare the main landing pages and campaign types to see where the shift occurred. An intake announcement may bring a wave of broad interest, while a separate search campaign may continue to attract applicants who meet the criteria. These situations call for different actions. Add raw counts alongside percentages so a reader can see whether a swing represents five people or fifty. Compare with a period that has a similar intake schedule where possible. If there is too little evidence to isolate the cause, propose the check that would distinguish the plausible explanations.

Keep comments tied to evidence

Put a short interpretation beside each chart or table. State what moved, what could plausibly have affected it and what you would check next. Give readers the date of any site release, budget change or event that might matter. Avoid treating an observation as a proven cause because it happened at the same time. If a change is clearly documented, such as a redirect that sent visitors to an outdated page, explain how you confirmed it. If the evidence is weaker, make that uncertainty visible and choose a reversible next step. A short note on data quality can be more useful than a confident explanation of a trend that cannot be reproduced.

Use the report as a working record

Finish with a few decisions the team can follow up. Assign each action an owner, a deadline and a check. One action might be to publish a clearer attendance summary, with a check that mobile visitors can find it before starting the registration form. Note the intended measure and when there should be enough activity to review it. Begin the following month's meeting with the previous actions and the evidence they produced. Keep records of decisions that were deferred and why. Over time, that history helps the team spot recurring problems and see which experiments are worth repeating. It also gives new colleagues context for metrics that would otherwise be hard to interpret.

Share the report before the meeting so readers have time to check anything unexpected. Use the discussion to resolve the largest uncertainty and agree what to do next. If an action needs more evidence, say exactly who will collect it and when it will be reviewed. Keep that decision beside the numbers that informed it. The next person reading the report should be able to understand both the finding and the trade-off the team considered.
