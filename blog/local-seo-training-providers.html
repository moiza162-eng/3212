Local SEO for training providers

Make the location part of the decision

A learner deciding whether to attend in person needs more than a city name. Put the teaching venue, delivery format and practical travel details on the course page. If enrolment takes place online but classes take place in Islington, state both clearly beside the registration link.

Review the public listings

Compare the website's contact information with the organisation's Google Business Profile, where eligible. Confirm the address, phone number, opening hours and destination URL with the team that manages them. If classes use a separate venue or follow different hours, explain the distinction on the site. Ask a colleague unfamiliar with the venue to find directions and report what was unclear.

Measure the actions that matter

Keep a record of local search themes and the pages that receive clicks for them. Track actions such as calls, directions clicks or completed enquiries where your tools support them. A change in direction requests might reflect a new cohort or an event, so annotate dates before drawing conclusions. Review results alongside the quality of enquiries and the questions the front desk receives.

Give each venue enough detail to stand alone

Start with the location where the learner actually needs to go. Include the street address, entrance information and any practical details the team can keep up to date. If the public office has different hours from the classroom, explain when each is available. If you offer both online and in-person sessions, state which parts happen where. Someone should be able to decide whether travel is feasible without opening several tabs. Link to directions from the course page and use a descriptive label rather than relying on a map alone. Check the final page on a phone, since people may use it outdoors while trying to identify the building.

Use location pages when the training differs

A page for a second venue can be helpful when it has its own schedule, facilities or access requirements. A page that only changes the place name leaves learners with little to act on. Ask the team at each site what a new attendee should know before travelling. Include only confirmed details about local services, travel and accessibility, and give readers a way to ask about individual needs. Once the venue pages are accurate, use internal links from the relevant courses and location directory to connect them. Check that old event pages do not present a previous venue as the current address.

Plan for changes before they happen

Agree who can update public listings, the course website and automated joining messages. Keep a short record of which pages and profiles need attention when hours or locations change. For a temporary move, update the pages students are most likely to find and include the dates the change applies to. Review any training calendar or PDF that might still circulate independently. Ask a colleague to start from a search engine and find the right entrance for the next class. Their route may uncover an older listing or a document the team missed.

Connect visibility with learner needs

Create a simple monthly view of clicks to location and course pages, relevant searches and measurable actions. If views rise but calls are mostly about where to go, review the landing pages for unclear directions. If enquiries repeatedly come from outside your service area, explain the location and attendance requirements near the enquiry button. Look at seasonal changes and cohort launches when interpreting trends. Where data is sparse, ask staff to collect a short list of recurring questions. Local search work is most valuable when it improves the information a learner needs to make a decision. Revisit that information at the start of each intake and soon after any change. Keep the next review date on the same list as the hours and contact details.

Before calling the review complete, ask someone outside the organisation to confirm the location of the next class using only public information. Give them the name of a course and observe which page they trust first. Their route can expose a confusing venue label or a link to directions that would otherwise look perfectly clear to someone who knows the area well.
